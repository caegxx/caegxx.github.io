A regional route can sell out around a holiday and struggle to cover its flight costs several weeks later. Keeping the holiday schedule throughout the year may waste capacity, while removing too many departures can make the remaining service less useful. The planning task is to decide how the product should change across the year and what those changes do to the airline's total result.

For a Southeast Asian network, that assessment needs to follow the particular source markets, travel purposes, and operating conditions of each route. A regional label such as winter peak gives too little information to support aircraft allocation. The useful starting point is a calendar of journeys and margins, linked to schedules the airline can actually operate.

## 1 Build a calendar for the market in both directions

Map school holidays, festivals, business activity, events, and weather relevant to each market. Check the dates for the year being planned. Holidays that move between calendar months can make a year-on-year monthly comparison misleading even when the underlying travel behaviour is similar.

Separate travel direction and purpose. Residents leaving before a holiday and visitors arriving for a resort stay may need different dates. A strong departure can be paired with a weak return, and inbound tourists may require a longer stay than a business traveller.

Weather also varies within the region. Singapore's meteorological service describes two monsoon seasons, inter-monsoon periods, and changes in rainfall within those periods. Thailand's government climate explanation notes that the northeast monsoon can bring rain to the southern Gulf coast while producing generally drier conditions in upper Thailand. \[1\]\[2\]

Those references establish why a single regional weather calendar is inadequate. They do not establish an airline demand curve. Build the commercial forecast from route-level evidence, and use local climate information to test destination appeal and operating assumptions separately.

## 2 Put monthly observations on a comparable basis

Passenger totals need a denominator. Compare passengers per operating day, passengers per flight, offered capacity, and net revenue alongside the monthly total. A lower count can reflect fewer days or fewer departures as well as weaker demand.

Changi Airport Group's published figures show 6.160 million passenger movements in January 2025 and 5.440 million in February 2025. \[3\] February's total was about 11.7% lower. Dividing by 31 and 28 days respectively gives approximately 198,710 and 194,286 movements per day, a difference of about 2.2%.

The daily adjustment changes the comparison substantially. It does not explain all remaining differences or remove holiday effects. Airport movements also combine many markets and include different types of traffic; they cannot be transferred directly into the forecast for an individual regional route.

For the airline's own history, identify capacity changes, cancellations, fare promotions, and unavailable inventory. An observed passenger count on a full flight may understate demand. A weak count after widespread disruption may describe an interrupted product rather than normal customer preference.

Use comparable weeks around major events where practical, and keep the original calendar dates visible. This preserves the evidence while allowing a more useful comparison.

## 3 Forecast revenue as well as occupancy

A seasonal plan needs both passenger volume and the margin those passengers generate. Peak demand can raise willingness to pay, while advance group commitments can fill seats at a lower net return. Neither relationship should be assumed without examining the commercial terms.

Forecast each direction by travel period and customer segment. Keep net fares, ancillary revenue, distribution expenses, and passenger-related costs consistent with the route model. If revenue is recorded per passenger journey, translate it correctly into the flown sectors being costed.

Examine booking lead times. A flight that appears weak eight weeks before departure may normally book late, while another market may depend on groups committed several months ahead. Compare bookings at the same lead time and allow for cancellations and changes before interpreting the difference.

Discounts can attract additional traffic, but some customers would have booked at a higher fare. Evaluate the change in total departure revenue and contribution. An increase in occupancy alone cannot show whether a promotion improved the result.

## 4 Show what each part of the year contributes

Consider a wholly hypothetical 70-seat service operating three round trips weekly. For simplicity, divide a 52-week planning year into 20 peak weeks, 16 shoulder weeks, and 16 low weeks. These periods are illustrative and do not represent particular months or destinations.

Each round trip contains two one-way flights. Assume additional flight costs of USD 2,500 per sector throughout the year. The passenger margin below is net revenue after passenger-related expenses, before those flight costs. Boardings are the average per one-way flight across both directions.

| **Period** | **Weeks** | **One-way flights** | **Boardings per flight** | **Margin per boarding** | **Period contribution** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Peak | 20 | 120 | 48 | USD 85 | USD 189,600 |
| Shoulder | 16 | 96 | 37 | USD 75 | USD 26,400 |
| Low | 16 | 96 | 26 | USD 65 | USD -77,760 |
| Full year | 52 | 312 | Varies | Varies | **USD 138,240** |

For example, a low-period flight earns 26 times USD 65, or USD 1,690, before its USD 2,500 flight cost. Its contribution is negative USD 810. Across 96 flights, the low period consumes USD 77,760 of the contribution earned elsewhere.

The annual total remains positive before station commitments, fleet costs, and company overhead. That supports further analysis of the annual service, but it does not show that every departure is the best use of the aircraft. An annual average can conceal the decision that needs attention.

## 5 Reforecast demand when reducing frequency

Removing a third of the flights does not leave demand unchanged automatically. Some travellers move to the remaining days; others choose a competitor, change destination, or abandon the trip. Retained customers may also face different fares.

Suppose the example reduces low-period flying to two round trips weekly. There are now 64 one-way flights in those 16 weeks. Assume average boardings rise to 32, with the same USD 65 passenger margin and USD 2,500 flight cost.

Each low-period flight then contributes negative USD 420, and the period loses USD 26,880. The full-year contribution rises to USD 189,120, an improvement of USD 50,880. However, low-period boardings fall from 2,496 to 2,048. The forecast explicitly loses 448 boardings while concentrating some of the remaining demand.

Those numbers describe a scenario, not a general effect of reducing frequency. Check whether the proposed days permit useful lengths of stay, maintain essential connections, and match the demand that remains. Two poorly chosen frequencies may produce a different result from two well-timed ones.

## 6 Compare a seasonal pause with the full cost of restarting

A pause removes the low-period flight contribution from the example. Peak and shoulder flying together contribute USD 216,000. If stopping and restarting adds USD 16,000, the resulting annual contribution is USD 200,000 under the assumptions below.

| **Annual pattern in the illustration** | **Annual contribution** | **Change from year-round baseline** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Three round trips weekly all year | **USD 138,240** | Baseline |
| Two round trips weekly in low period | USD 189,120 | USD 50,880 higher |
| Pause in low period and pay restart cost | USD 200,000 | USD 61,760 higher |

This comparison assumes all other continuing costs are identical, the full USD 2,500 per cancelled sector is avoidable, and peak and shoulder demand is unaffected. The USD 16,000 includes only additional costs caused by stopping and restarting. Any station commitments that remain payable through a pause still need to be included in the full annual budget.

Challenge those assumptions before choosing the largest number. A handling minimum may continue during a pause. Reopening may require training or positioning. Customers and agents may shift to a competitor and fail to return at the forecast level. Lost connecting contribution elsewhere in the network may also change the ranking.

The paused aircraft still has an economic role. Identify whether it can earn more on another route, undergo planned maintenance, or provide useful recovery cover. Include the relevant alternative contribution and any transfer costs in a network comparison.

## 7 Check whether the seasonal plan can be executed

Commercial flexibility depends on permissions, contracts, and airport capacity. An intended return date needs the required operational arrangements in place. A previous season's usable departure time should not be treated as an unconditional promise for the next one.

IATA explains that historic precedence for slots at coordinated airports is conditional on the applicable rules, including utilisation, and that unused slots should be returned promptly. It also distinguishes slots from route rights and other operating permissions. \[4\] Check the current coordinator requirements and relevant national rules before approving reductions or a seasonal pause.

Match the aircraft plan with crew availability, maintenance, and station capability. More flying in a strong period can increase the need for recovery capacity. Moving an aircraft to another market may create a different crew base, positioning requirement, or contract commitment.

Where the weak period offers a maintenance opportunity, compare the actual maintenance window with the commercially attractive operating dates. A maintenance plan that runs into the return season can remove some of the revenue the seasonal strategy was intended to protect.

## 8 Make decisions before weak bookings become a disruption

Set decision dates around commercial lead times and the point at which costs become committed. The appropriate review date differs between a group-led market and one that books close to departure. Waiting for near-final bookings can leave too little time to sell an alternative schedule responsibly.

At each review, compare bookings at equivalent lead times, expected net revenue, remaining distribution opportunities, and the contribution of operating versus changing the plan. Keep forecast confidence visible. A predictable seasonal trough and a sudden market deterioration require different interpretations.

Communicate a chosen seasonal pattern consistently through reservation systems, agents, station arrangements, and customer information. Repeated late cancellations can change future demand and undermine the assumptions used to justify the next launch.

After the period ends, compare the forecast with both flown results and the consequences of changes. Record whether customers shifted days as expected, which costs were avoided, and how much demand returned after a pause. That evidence improves the next annual plan more than the final load factor alone.

## Closing

Seasonality management connects the demand calendar with the contribution and operating requirements of each schedule. The best annual pattern may include different frequencies, a limited season, or a continuous service whose weaker weeks are justified by wider value. The decision becomes clearer when every option uses a revised demand forecast and a complete account of the costs and consequences it changes.
