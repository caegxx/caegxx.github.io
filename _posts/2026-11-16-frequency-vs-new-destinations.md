An airline has enough aircraft time for three additional round trips each week. Sales wants a new destination. Revenue management would rather put the flights into a market the airline already serves. Both proposals can produce a credible load-factor forecast, and both can be wrong about what the extra flying earns.

The decision depends on the change in the airline's result. An additional frequency can improve the existing product while moving some customers between its own departures. A new route can attract additional traffic while introducing station costs and a longer period of uncertainty.

For a small regional carrier, the useful comparison starts with the same aircraft window and the same season. It then asks which option produces the stronger incremental network contribution within an operable schedule.

## 1 Define the aircraft window before comparing markets

Three round trips are not interchangeable just because the frequency is the same. The candidate routes may require different block hours, ground time, crew duties, and overnight arrangements. A departure that fits an empty box on a weekly plan may prevent a maintenance opportunity or make the next morning's rotation less reliable.

Specify the resource being allocated: operating days, available hours, aircraft capability, crew availability, and the required return to base. Include both directions and the turn at the destination. Then identify the actual alternatives that fit.

Use the same evaluation period for both options. Compare the proposed season, including its weak weeks, and show any station setup expenditure separately from recurring costs. A mature-market forecast for the additional frequency should not be compared with only the new destination's best holiday departures.

Keeping the current programme is also an option. Unused aircraft time can have a recovery or maintenance value. The added flying must improve the result sufficiently to justify the resources and operating commitments it consumes.

## 2 Identify which part of the existing product is missing

Frequency can improve several different aspects of a journey. Adding a missing weekday broadens travel-date choice. Adding an evening return may enable a business trip that was impractical before. A second departure can create access to a connecting bank or reduce the wait after a cancellation.

Those effects depend on the schedule. An additional flight close to an existing departure may divide the same demand, while one at a different time can serve customers whose preferred journey was previously unavailable.

Research by Mark Hansen and Yi Liu compares frequency-based market-share models with models based on schedule delay, the difference between desired and available departure times. Their analysis is a useful reminder that the relationship between frequency and market share depends on how traveller choice is represented. It does not provide a universal uplift percentage for a regional route. \[1\]

Before recommending an increase, identify the demand currently being lost. Useful evidence includes constrained high-fare departures, rejected group requests, corporate travel requirements, competitor timings, and observed journeys that use another airport or connection. A full flight in a holiday week is evidence of a particular constraint; it does not establish demand for another year-round frequency.

## 3 Forecast the whole route after the increase

The proposed additional departures cannot be evaluated independently of the flights already operating. Some passengers will move to a more convenient day or time. Others may pay a different fare, and inventory controls may change across the route.

Classify the expected bookings by their effect on the airline:

- Additional traffic includes customers captured from competitors, other transport modes, or journeys that become attractive because the schedule improves.

- Transferred traffic moves between the carrier's existing and added departures. It improves the new flight's occupancy without necessarily increasing total passengers.

- Network changes include new connecting demand, displacement on other sectors, and any loss caused by retiming an existing service.

For the frequency option, produce a forecast for every departure in the resulting programme. Then compare its total revenue and relevant costs with the programme that would operate without the increase.

Suppose the airline currently offers four round trips a week and a competitor offers seven. Increasing to seven changes the airline's frequency share from four of eleven departures to seven of fourteen. That is a capacity and schedule observation. It does not prove that passenger share will become 50%, because fares, timings, distribution, loyalty, and product differences still matter.

A choice model or quality-of-service index can help organise those attributes. Its assumptions should be calibrated where evidence permits and challenged where it does not. A model output is a forecast conditional on those inputs.

## 4 Give the new destination an equally complete business case

A new destination may diversify the network and reach demand that existing flights cannot serve. It also needs a case for the actual proposed product: operating days, times, fare, airport access, distribution, and service frequency.

Distinguish the size of the underlying market from the passengers the airline expects to capture. An existing flow through a hub shows that people travel between the cities; it does not show that all of them will switch to a three-weekly direct service. Some need daily flexibility, and others value the incumbent's network or commercial conditions.

Assess both directions. A strong outbound tour programme may leave the return weak, and a forecast based on unique customers can be inconsistent with a cost model based on flown sectors. Keep passenger journeys and passenger boardings explicit.

Add the costs and commitments specific to opening the station. These can include local support, training, systems, sales arrangements, and contract minimums. Identify which are one-off, which recur, and which remain payable if the trial is stopped. Airport or tourism support should appear separately with its conditions and collection assumptions. IATA's destination-development guidance discusses fee reductions, risk-sharing, and marketing support as different instruments; they should not be treated as equivalent revenue. \[2\]

## 5 Compare two uses of the same weekly capacity

The following example is entirely hypothetical. It uses a 70-seat aircraft, six additional one-way flights each week, and 90 minutes of block time per flight for both options. Each option therefore adds three round trips, 420 offered seats, and nine block hours weekly. These are calculation assumptions, not current route costs or demand estimates.

For simplicity, each additional passenger boarding earns USD 100 in net fare and ancillary revenue and incurs USD 10 of passenger-related cost. The resulting margin before flight-level costs is USD 90. Cargo, subsidies, capital costs, and wider network benefits are excluded from this illustration.

| **Weekly item** | **Add frequency on an existing route** | **Open a new destination** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Additional one-way flights | 6 | 6 |
| Boardings forecast on the added flights | 294 | 252 |
| Boardings transferred from existing flights | 84 | 0 assumed |
| Additional boardings across the carrier's programme | 210 | 252 |
| Margin from those additional boardings at USD 90 each | USD 18,900 | USD 22,680 |
| Additional flight costs at USD 2,400 per flight | USD 14,400 | USD 14,400 |
| Additional recurring station cost for this option | USD 0 assumed | USD 6,000 |
| Revenue reduction on previously carried traffic | USD 800 | USD 0 assumed |
| Change in weekly contribution | **USD 3,700** | **USD 2,280** |

The frequency option's 294 boardings comprise 210 additional boardings and 84 transferred from existing flights. Counting all 294 as additional would overstate its benefit. The USD 800 captures an assumed reduction in revenue on previously carried traffic; their total passenger-related costs are assumed unchanged by the transfer.

The new route carries more additional passengers, yet its recurring station commitment reduces the weekly result. Setup expenditure would need a further line. Neither result demonstrates that the airline covers all company or fleet costs.

The zero entries are assumptions, not shortcuts that should carry into a real model. An existing station may need a longer shift or additional handling cover. A new destination may divert customers from another route in the same airline's network. Those effects must be included when present.

## 6 Test the assumptions that can reverse the choice

The frequency option leads by USD 1,420 per week in the base case. That is a forecast difference worth testing rather than treating as a precise ranking.

If it attracts five fewer additional passengers on each of the six added flights, weekly margin falls by USD 2,700. Its contribution drops from USD 3,700 to USD 1,000, below the new-route base case. The explanation for additional demand therefore matters more than the attractive occupancy forecast for the new flights.

Conversely, if the new station's recurring cost is USD 4,000 rather than USD 6,000, its contribution becomes USD 4,280. A better contract could reverse the decision without changing the passenger forecast.

Also test fare dilution, fuel, currency, launch delay, and the competitor's likely response. Apply shared shocks consistently. A fuel increase affecting both options should not be charged to one and omitted from the other.

For uncertainty that cannot be estimated credibly, state the condition required for the proposal to work. For example, identify the minimum incremental demand or maximum station commitment consistent with an acceptable result. That gives management something observable to negotiate or verify.

## 7 Confirm that the useful timings are available

The commercial comparison depends on the schedule customers will actually receive. IATA's slot guidance distinguishes airport capacity allocation from route rights and other permissions. A suitable slot also depends on the airport's capacity at the requested time. \[3\]

For an additional frequency, confirm the operating days and times that produce the forecast benefit. If the business case relies on an evening return but only a midday slot is available, the demand forecast needs revision. For a new destination, include the approval sequence and the station's readiness in the realistic launch date.

Compare aircraft utilisation after accounting for the rest of the rotation. Two options requiring nine block hours can create very different usable gaps, overnight positions, or recovery margins. Crew and maintenance constraints may eliminate a financially attractive pattern before it becomes an executable choice.

The final comparison should use an operable version of each proposal. Keep any commercially preferable but unavailable schedule as a separate scenario.

## 8 Design a trial that can answer the question

A limited increase can help test demand, but the trial needs an evaluation period long enough to observe bookings and travel. Set the review dates before launch and distinguish early launch effects from repeat demand.

For a frequency increase, monitor total route passengers and net revenue, movement between departures, achieved fares, and contribution across the full programme. For a new destination, add station costs, distribution progress, and evidence that customers return after introductory offers.

Agree in advance which outcomes would support retention, retiming, resizing, or withdrawal. Keep the approval and contract implications of each action in view. A trial with no practical exit can become a long commitment despite being described as temporary.

## Closing

An additional frequency is attractive when it improves a travel product that customers value and earns enough incremental contribution after transfers and dilution. A new destination is attractive when its additional market and network value justify the station commitment and demand risk.

The planner's task is to compare both using the same resources, period, and accounting boundary. The stronger proposal is the one that remains worthwhile after those assumptions are tested and the schedule is made operable.
