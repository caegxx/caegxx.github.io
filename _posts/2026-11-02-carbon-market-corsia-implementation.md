Carbon compliance enters an airline through several departments at once. Operations holds the fuel records. Regulatory affairs manages reporting. Finance needs an estimate of the liability. Procurement receives offers for credits whose eligibility may be harder to establish than their price.

The resulting problem is operational: turning a flight record into verified emissions, a correctly scoped obligation, and documented compliance. Buying a carbon product is only one step in that chain.

This article focuses on CORSIA as it stands in November 2026, near the end of its first phase. The relevant ICAO policy resolution is now [Assembly Resolution A42-22](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/environmental-protection/Assembly42/Resolution-A42-22_CORSIA.pdf), alongside Annex 16, Volume IV and the current implementation documents.

## 1. Carbon markets do not all sell the same instrument

An emissions trading system typically uses allowances under a defined cap. An offset credit represents an emissions reduction or removal under a crediting programme. A voluntary corporate claim and a mandatory aviation obligation can involve different eligibility rules even when both are expressed in tonnes of CO2 equivalent.

CORSIA uses eligible emissions units for its offsetting obligation. The airline must establish that the specific units qualify for its compliance period; a generic environmental certificate does not establish that fact. ICAO's [eligible emissions units page](https://www.icao.int/CORSIA/corsia-eligible-emissions-units) is the starting point for this check.

For purchasing controls, keep four identities separate: the supplier, the crediting programme, the underlying activity, and the actual registry units being delivered. A recognised supplier name cannot stand in for the other three.

The practical question is precise: can this identified unit be used for this operator's obligation, for this period, under the applicable requirements?

## 2. Monitoring and offsetting have different boundaries

CORSIA monitoring, reporting, and verification, or MRV, has applied since 2019 to qualifying operators. [EASA's CORSIA overview](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/environment/icao-carbon-offsetting-and-reduction-scheme-international-aviation-corsia) identifies the general threshold: more than 10,000 tonnes of annual CO2 from international flights using aeroplanes with maximum take-off mass above 5,700 kg. Applicable exclusions and the operator's attribution to an administering State also need checking.

Offsetting is narrower. For a given year, the participating States determine the State pairs covered by the offsetting requirement. An operator can have reporting duties for international flying that does not create a CORSIA offsetting obligation.

This matters when a network crosses both participating and non-participating States. Classify actual international flight legs by the relevant State pair. A passenger's ticket origin and final destination do not replace the physical legs in the emissions record.

For example, a passenger travelling from country A through country H to country B generates two flown legs. The airline needs the relevant emissions and coverage assessment for A–H and H–B. Treating the entire journey as a single A–B movement would distort the scope.

## 3. What changes in 2027

| Phase | Years | Participation basis |
|---|---|---|
| Pilot | 2021–2023 | Voluntary State participation |
| First | 2024–2026 | Voluntary State participation |
| Second | 2027–2035 | Aviation-activity criteria, with specified exemptions and voluntary participation |

The second phase does not mean every international route becomes subject to offsetting. Resolution A42-22 retains exemptions involving aviation activity and specified State categories. ICAO publishes the applicable annual [CORSIA States for Chapter 3 State Pairs](https://www.icao.int/CORSIA/corsia-states-chapter-3-state-pairs) document.

The first-phase baseline is 85% of the corresponding 2019 covered international aviation emissions. It is a sectoral baseline for the relevant coverage, rather than a rule requiring each airline to offset 15% of all its emissions. The current framework uses the sectoral growth factor for annual calculations through 2032; the later period introduces an individual component.

For planning, version the State-pair list by emissions year. A list used to assess 2026 flying must not silently become the input for 2027. Keep the document edition and retrieval date with the coverage table.

## 4. Build the calculation from the covered emissions

For the current sectoral calculation, the conceptual relationship is:

**Annual requirement before eligible-fuel reductions = covered operator emissions × applicable annual sector growth factor.**

Use the factor published by ICAO and the requirement determined by the administering State. The [ICAO Central Registry](https://www.icao.int/CORSIA/CCR) provides implementation data. A preliminary company forecast should remain labelled as an estimate, particularly before the annual factor and verified emissions are final.

The following figures are entirely hypothetical; the 10% factor is an assumption, not a published value for 2026:

| Step | Illustrative value |
|---|---:|
| International emissions in the example reporting scope | 50,000 tonnes CO2 |
| Subset on State pairs subject to offsetting | 30,000 tonnes CO2 |
| Assumed sector growth factor | 10% |
| Requirement before eligible-fuel reductions | 3,000 tonnes |
| Assumed verified eligible-fuel reduction | 400 tonnes |
| Indicative remaining amount | 2,600 tonnes |

At an assumed USD 20 per eligible unit representing one tonne, the purchase amount would be USD 52,000, before fees and other compliance expenses. Neither the price nor the volume is a market forecast.

For budgeting, vary covered emissions, the growth factor, eligible-fuel reductions, and unit cost separately. A single carbon-price assumption cannot explain the full liability.

## 5. Eligibility belongs in the purchase specification

ICAO approves programmes with defined scopes and conditions. That does not mean every credit from an approved programme qualifies. Check the relevant compliance period, eligible activity and vintage conditions, registry designation, and requirements addressing double claiming. Use the current approved document rather than a supplier's summary of an older edition.

A purchase specification should identify:

- The compliance period the units must serve.
- The applicable programme and eligibility conditions.
- Required registry evidence and unit identifiers.
- Delivery, cancellation, and reporting responsibilities.
- Remedies if delivered units do not meet the contractual eligibility specification.

This is a compliance procurement decision with price exposure, rather than a reason for the airline to take an unrelated speculative carbon position. Treasury should understand payment timing and counterparty exposure; regulatory affairs should approve the eligibility basis.

Keep the evidence after purchase. An invoice shows expenditure. Registry records and the required verification demonstrate what happened to the units.

## 6. Eligible fuels require their own evidence chain

Using a fuel marketed as sustainable does not automatically establish a CORSIA reduction claim. ICAO's [eligible fuels framework](https://www.icao.int/CORSIA/corsia-eligible-fuels) covers sustainability certification and associated implementation documents. The [life-cycle methodology](https://www.icao.int/CORSIA/fuels-lifecycle) evaluates emissions across the fuel pathway, rather than treating the fuel as having zero emissions at the aircraft.

The operator needs a supportable fuel quantity, applicable life-cycle emissions value, certification evidence, and verified claim. Prevent the same reduction being attributed more than once.

Keep three accounting questions separate: physical fuel consumption, the eligible life-cycle reduction claimed under CORSIA, and the purchase premium paid for that fuel. Combining them into one fuel-cost adjustment makes both the emissions review and the financial review harder.

For route analysis, show the gross fuel expense and the carbon-related effects explicitly. Do not assume that the premium is automatically recovered through a lower offsetting requirement.

## 7. Treat the calendar as a compliance project

Annual emissions reporting and the three-year offsetting compliance cycle are different clocks. Data collection and independent verification cannot wait until the unit-cancellation deadline.

ICAO's [2026 TAB briefing](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/environmental-protection/CORSIA/TAB/2026/2026-TAB-webinar_20260218.pdf) identifies 31 January 2028 as the cancellation deadline for the 2024–2026 period. Annex implementation provisions include a later-notification condition; the operator should confirm its exact deadline with its administering State. The [Dutch Emissions Authority](https://www.emissionsauthority.nl/topics/ets-aviation/reporting) also describes the subsequent verified cancellation-report deadline of 30 April 2028 in its implementation guidance.

Build backwards from those milestones. Allow time for emissions reconciliation, verifier questions, State notification, purchasing authority, registry access, cancellation, and the cancellation report itself. National reporting procedures can add requirements and should be tracked separately.

An emissions register should link flight identifier, date, aircraft, origin, destination, fuel data source, method, coverage year, corrections, and verification status. Reconcile it with operated-flight records. A cancelled flight left in one dataset or a positioning flight missing from another can create a systematic error.

## 8. Bring the cost into decisions without distorting them

For network planning, allocate an estimated CORSIA expense using a documented relationship to the covered emissions, then reconcile to the compliance total. Maintain separate lines for verification, administration, and procurement costs where material.

Compare fuel-saving initiatives on the same basis. A routing improvement can reduce fuel spend and covered emissions, but its benefit depends on the actual operation and applicable obligation. A full passenger cabin does not eliminate the aircraft's emissions or its compliance cost.

Three management controls are especially useful: one approved coverage table per year, one reconciled emissions dataset, and an eligibility sign-off before unit purchases. They address different failure points and should have named owners.

## Closing

CORSIA implementation connects operating records with regulatory calculations and financial execution. The strongest process can explain every transition: which flights were counted, which emissions were covered, how the obligation was determined, and which eligible units were cancelled.

That evidence chain is the foundation for a reliable carbon budget and a completed compliance cycle.
