A regional airline can operate ten destinations and sell journeys to a hundred. The additional reach comes from partners, but the route map tells only a small part of the story. A signed agreement does not establish that an agent can issue the ticket, a bag can transfer, or a stranded passenger can reach someone authorised to rebook the journey.

The previous article examined [airline revenue management](/posts/airline-revenue-management-fundamentals/). Partnerships add another dimension to that problem: deciding which connecting passengers to accept, how much revenue each carrier keeps, and who pays when the connection fails.

For a Southeast Asian regional carrier, the useful starting point is the journey being sold. The legal agreement, inventory, airport process, and settlement arrangement must all support that same journey.

## 1. Interline and codeshare solve different problems

An interline arrangement enables carriers to accept and handle traffic across their services under an agreed framework. The practical product may include a through ticket and baggage transfer, subject to the agreements, systems, and local requirements. A codeshare places one carrier's marketing code on a flight operated by another carrier. The marketing airline sells the flight under its code; the operating airline performs it. [IATA's interline framework](https://www.iata.org/en/programs/airline-distribution/retailing/future-of-interline/multilateral-interline-framework/) and the [US Department of Transportation's codeshare overview](https://www.transportation.gov/policy/aviation-policy/licensing/code-sharing) explain these foundations.

| Arrangement | What changes for the customer | What the airline must establish |
|---|---|---|
| Interline | An agreed way to combine participating carriers' services | Ticket acceptance, servicing, baggage arrangements, and settlement |
| Codeshare | A partner's code appears on the operating flight | Approved marketing scope, inventory access, disclosure, and service responsibilities |
| Deeper commercial cooperation | Potential coordination beyond individual bookings | A separate commercial structure and any necessary competition approvals |

These are overlapping arrangements, rather than mandatory steps up a ladder. Interline can exist without codeshare. A codeshare does not automatically give either party permission to coordinate prices across its wider network.

Consider a fictional airline A operating a regional feeder and airline B operating an onward international sector. An interline journey might show A's code on the feeder and B's code onward. Under codeshare, B might also market the feeder under its own code. The aircraft and connection can remain identical; the distribution and commercial relationship change.

## 2. Begin with specific origin-and-destination markets

A partnership proposal should start with a short list of journeys that become meaningfully better. Examples might include a secondary ASEAN city gaining one-stop access to a long-haul market, or an overseas visitor gaining a ticketed connection to a regional destination.

For each candidate journey, compare total elapsed time, frequency, day-of-week coverage, expected fare, baggage conditions, and recovery options against competing itineraries. A partner with a famous brand can still contribute little if its departures consistently miss the regional arrival bank.

Then distinguish three kinds of demand:

- **Incremental traffic:** passengers the carrier would probably not have carried without the partnership.
- **Transferred traffic:** existing customers who now book through the partner, potentially at a different net revenue.
- **Displacing traffic:** connecting passengers who occupy seats that could have earned more from another booking.

This distinction prevents a common evaluation error: treating every partner booking as additional revenue. The relevant comparison is the airline's outcome with and without the booking, including what happens on busy departures.

A small launch scope is often easier to evaluate than a network-wide announcement. Two well-designed connecting markets can reveal pricing, airport, and servicing problems before those problems spread across dozens of combinations.

## 3. Separate the agreement from the technical connection

IATA's Multilateral Interline Traffic Agreement, or MITA, provides a contractual framework; it does not connect every participant automatically. The carriers still need the relevant relationship and technical capability. IATA's [implementation paper](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/e7a533819be440edbb1e49da96e0f2a8/industry-restart-new-partnerships-in-interline-framework.pdf) distinguishes MITA participation, bilateral concurrence, electronic ticket connectivity, and separate electronic miscellaneous document capability.

For implementation, maintain a record of which capabilities actually work between the two carriers:

| Capability | A useful acceptance test |
|---|---|
| Booking and availability | The intended itinerary prices and books in the intended sales channel |
| Electronic ticketing | The ticket issues on the authorised validating carrier and the operating carrier can use its coupon |
| Changes and refunds | Agents can complete a voluntary change and process the agreed refund scenario |
| Airport handling | Check-in staff can recognise the onward journey and apply the documented baggage process |
| Ancillaries | A seat or bag purchase remains correctly associated with the passenger after a change |
| Irregular operations | Staff can rebook a missed connection using the agreed authority and workflow |

One successful booking is insufficient. Test both directions, different sales channels, and a schedule change after ticketing. If only one direction is commercially or technically enabled, describe the product that way.

## 4. Revenue sharing determines whether the feed is useful

The customer buys a journey, but each operating airline needs an attributable revenue amount. A special prorate agreement, or SPA, can establish bilateral revenue-sharing terms. Multilateral prorate arrangements provide another framework; IATA's [Prorate Agency materials](https://www.iata.org/en/services/finance/prorate-manuals/) describe their role in allocating interline revenue.

An SPA negotiation is about more than a headline amount per passenger. Define the applicable routes, booking classes, currencies, directionality, taxes, surcharges, children and infants, refunds, and involuntary rerouting. Specify how the agreement changes when one sector is cancelled or the itinerary is modified.

The following example is constructed for illustration, with no connection to an actual carrier's agreement:

| Item | Illustrative amount per passenger |
|---|---:|
| Amount allocated to the regional sector, excluding taxes | USD 72 |
| Distribution and payment expense | USD 4 |
| Additional passenger handling and servicing cost | USD 6 |
| Contribution before displacement and flight-level costs | USD 62 |

On a departure with otherwise empty seats, USD 62 can be useful incremental contribution. If admitting that booking displaces a passenger expected to contribute USD 95 on the same seat, the comparison reverses. Fixed flight costs still need recovery across the service as a whole.

Evaluate the agreement by departure and season, rather than applying an annual average to every seat. Capacity controls and SPA terms need to work together.

## 5. Codeshare adds inventory and regulatory questions

Two familiar commercial structures are free-sale and block-space arrangements. Under free sale, the marketing carrier accesses agreed inventory made available by the operating carrier. Under block space, capacity is allocated under agreed purchase and release conditions. Whether unused capacity creates a financial obligation depends on the contract; the label alone does not settle the question.

The planner needs answers to practical questions. Who closes availability on a constrained departure? When can blocked seats return to general inventory? What happens after an aircraft downgrade? Are booking-class mappings consistent with the product being promised?

Regulatory review is a separate workstream. Check bilateral provisions, each carrier's underlying authority, applicable codeshare permissions, and any required safety assessment. US rules provide a concrete example: DOT describes authorisation requirements and FAA review of the US carrier's foreign-partner safety audit. That example should not be copied as a universal ASEAN procedure.

Customer disclosure also belongs in launch testing. DOT's [passenger protection rule](https://www.transportation.gov/airconsumer/final-rule-enhancing-airline-passenger-protections-iii-word) addresses disclosure of the actual operator in the US sales context. Other markets have their own requirements. The commercial brand must not obscure who will operate the flight.

## 6. A legal connection needs an operationally credible buffer

Minimum connecting time, or MCT, is specific to the airport and applicable connection characteristics. IATA's [MCT technical guide](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/638f0938b3dd451b872a1d8357755421/minimum-connecting-time-technical-guide_version-2.1.pdf) explains how carrier and flight-specific records affect itinerary construction.

Meeting the filed minimum establishes one condition for selling a connection. It does not establish that the connection will perform well through a wet-season afternoon or a recurring late inbound.

For a fictional example, suppose the applicable MCT is 60 minutes and the proposed connection provides 90 minutes. If the inbound arrives 45 minutes late, the remaining 45 minutes fall below that minimum. The analysis needs the arrival-delay distribution, terminal process, baggage transfer, and last onward departure, rather than the scheduled interval alone.

Recovery frequency can matter more than a small difference in scheduled elapsed time. Missing an onward flight with another departure in three hours is a different product risk from missing a twice-weekly service.

## 7. Put servicing and settlement into the launch plan

Before sales open, name the teams responsible for disruption notifications, rebooking, accommodation decisions, baggage tracing, and customer communication. Document spending authority and escalation contacts that work outside office hours. The agreement should allocate costs; station instructions should make those allocations usable.

Finance has its own acceptance gate. [IATA SIS](https://www.iata.org/en/services/finance/sis/airlines/) supports industry billing processes, while the [IATA Clearing House](https://www.iata.org/en/services/finance/clearinghouse/faqs/) provides settlement and netting. Billing and settlement are distinct functions. Participation in an industry platform does not replace commercial terms, reconciliation, or credit controls.

Run a complete sample from booking to uplift, billing, settlement, and exception handling. A partnership that creates bookings but produces disputed receivables has not completed implementation.

## Closing

For a regional carrier, a useful partnership produces journeys that customers can buy, airport teams can deliver, and finance teams can reconcile. Measure net contribution, connection success, baggage performance, and servicing cost alongside passenger volume.

Start with a small set of defensible markets. Prove the entire journey, including a disruption. Expand when the evidence supports expansion.
